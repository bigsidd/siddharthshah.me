The twelve rules

Pick a card. Turn it over for why I hold it, an example, and when it doesn't apply.

  1. 01

    Who is it for, and when?

    Name the person, the moment they hit the problem, and what they do about it today, even if that's nothing.

    Why. An idea with no person attached can only be liked or disliked. Once there's a person at a moment, there's a test: does this help them, then, more than what they already do?

    Example. "Add a stats page." A visitor deciding in ten seconds whether the project is alive needs one line: the date of the last release. I need something else: whether anyone uses it. Same words, two different builds.

    Fires when
    The issue doesn't say who it's for, when they hit the problem, or what they do today.
    Not when
    The person and the situation are stated, even in one line.
    From
    Jobs to be done, Clayton Christensen and Bob Moesta. Ash Maurya on existing alternatives.
  2. 02

    What are they really after?

    A request is someone's first guess at a fix. Solve the goal behind it. One loud request is one data point.

    Why. The goal comes back in other requests. The exact fix rarely does, and how loudly someone asks says nothing about how many others need it.

    Example. "Email me the test results every morning." The goal is probably "tell me when something breaks." An alert on failure does that, without a daily email to learn to ignore.

    Fires when
    The issue asks for a specific fix without saying what it's for.
    Not when
    The issue states both the goal and the fix, or is a bug with steps to reproduce.
    From
    Jobs to be done, Christensen and Moesta.
  3. 03

    Does it beat what they do now?

    The problem has to rank near the top of their list, and the new way has to clearly beat the old one. Judge that by what people have done, not what they say they'd do.

    Why. Almost anyone will agree a problem is real. Fewer will rank it above everything else on their plate, and fewer still will switch for a small improvement. "I'd definitely use that" is a guess about the future. What they did last month is evidence.

    Example. A faster way to run one command saves ten seconds a week. The current way works and nobody has complained. It's a real improvement that won't change anything.

    Fires when
    Nothing shows the current way is failing anyone.
    Not when
    Someone has actually hit the problem, or it blocks real work.
    From
    Rob Fitzpatrick, The Mom Test, on trusting past behavior. John Gourville on why small improvements don't get adopted.
  4. 04

    Know who you're not serving

    Know whose needs come first, why they'd pick this over the alternatives, and what you're happy to be worse at. Build what strengthens that. Turn down what pulls the product toward someone else.

    Why. Every product is a set of choices about who it's for. A feature that serves the wrong person still costs upkeep and blurs the reason anyone chose it. Saying yes to everyone is how a sharp tool becomes a generic one.

    Example. A command-line tool chosen because it's fast and has no setup gets a request for a plugin system and a config file. Both are reasonable. Both make it slower and harder to start, which is the opposite of why people picked it.

    Fires when
    A new feature pulls toward a user the project isn't for, or the project hasn't said who it's for.
    Not when
    Repairs, and features that strengthen the stated reason to use it.
    From
    Michael Porter: the essence of strategy is choosing what not to do.
  5. 05

    Most ideas don't work

    Most ideas don't improve what they set out to improve, mine included. Before building, say what someone will do differently because of this. If I can't, I don't build it.

    Why. When teams actually test their ideas, most make no difference or make things worse. An idea feeling useful to the person who had it tells you very little.

    Example. An animated header looks better, but I can't name anyone who'd do something differently because of it. A header showing the one command to try first might get someone to try it.

    Fires when
    The issue names no change in what anyone will do.
    Not when
    The issue says who will do what differently, or repairs something people already use.
    From
    Ronny Kohavi's research on controlled experiments.
  6. 06

    Cheapest answer first

    Before code, check whether a setting, a quick manual edit or a small test gets the same result. If it does, take it. If it gives up something that matters, build.

    Why. The cheapest code is the code nobody writes. But cheap only wins when it really does the job; a workaround that loses the point is just a slower way to build the wrong thing.

    Example. "Build a newsletter signup." A link and a click count for two weeks tells me whether anyone reading the site wants it. If nobody clicks, this version for these readers can wait. It doesn't prove nobody ever would.

    Fires when
    A setting, a quick manual edit or a small test would get the same result.
    Not when
    The shortcut gives up something that matters, or the same manual edit keeps recurring.
    From
    Alberto Savoia on testing an idea before building it.
  7. 07

    Rough, not hollow

    Ship early where real use will answer the question. Think through the details that make it feel right, and never cut the one the idea depends on.

    Why. Some questions only real use can answer, so polishing those first is guessing. Others you can reason through beforehand: what the person will feel at each step, and which detail creates that feeling. Ship without that detail and you've shipped a different, worse idea. "We'll add it later" rarely arrives.

    Example. A copy-to-clipboard button without the "Copied" confirmation leaves people clicking twice and wondering. The confirmation is the feature.

    Fires when
    A large issue is being cut down to size.
    Not when
    Small changes with nothing to cut.
    From
    37signals, Getting Real. Jason Cohen on simple, lovable, complete.
  8. 08

    Make the first step easy

    People act when they want it enough, it's easy enough to do, and something prompts them at that moment. If one is missing, more enthusiasm won't fix it.

    Why. When something goes unused, I check those three first. Each has a different fix. Setup that takes an hour isn't solved by a better pitch.

    Example. A tool that needs a config file before it does anything can lose people at the config file. A working default fixes that, where a better tutorial wouldn't.

    Fires when
    Someone has to adopt the thing, and the first step isn't defined.
    Not when
    Internal changes nobody has to adopt.
    From
    BJ Fogg's behavior model: motivation, ability and a prompt.
  9. 09

    Say what should change

    Every piece of work names what should be different afterward, and how I'd see it.

    Why. "Shipped" is the middle of the story. Without a stated result, nobody can tell whether it worked, so nobody learns anything. The result doesn't have to be a number; it has to be something I could check.

    Example. "Make the tests faster" can't be finished. "Get the test run under 20 seconds" can.

    Fires when
    The issue has no result anyone could check.
    Not when
    The issue states a checkable result.
    From
    Josh Seiden, Outcomes Over Output.
  10. 10

    Only ask what changes the decision

    Ask a question only if the answers would lead to different decisions. Otherwise decide.

    Why. "Let's get more information" feels careful. Often it's a way to avoid making the call. A good question is one where each possible answer sends the work somewhere different: build, refuse, or build something else.

    Example. "Bump Node to 22." Asking why 22 changes nothing. "Add login": password, email link or Google are three different builds, so that question earns its place.

    Fires when
    A question is about to be asked whose answers would all lead to the same call.
    Not when
    Different answers would lead to different decisions.
    From
    Ronald Howard, "Information Value Theory" (1966).
  11. 11Weighs

    Play it forward

    Before approving a change, picture how the person reacts, then what happens after that. Write the prediction down so it can be wrong.

    Why. It's easy to judge a change from my own chair. The first effect is usually the one I intended. The second is where the surprises are.

    Example. A bot that auto-closes stale issues keeps the tracker tidy. Played forward, people stop filing issues because theirs vanish, and the tracker looks healthy while the feedback dries up.

    Fires when
    A change affects how people behave.
    Not when
    Never cited as the reason for a call.
    From
    Second-order thinking, as written up by Farnam Street. The pre-mortem is Gary Klein's.
  12. 12Weighs

    Rank what matters, then choose, and say why

    When rules or options pull in different directions, rank what this decision has to get right, then pick. Every yes or no carries its reason. "Not now, I'll decide Friday" is an answer.

    Why. Pros and cons go in circles because everyone weighs them differently. Agreeing on what matters most usually settles it, and a better third option often shows up while ranking. A call without its reason can't be checked or learned from.

    Example. "Which database?" turns into a feature fight. "It has to run on a $5 server, and I have to be able to back it up in one command" ends it.

    Fires when
    Rules or options pull in different directions.
    Not when
    Never cited as the reason for a call.
    From
    Kepner-Tregoe on decision analysis.
